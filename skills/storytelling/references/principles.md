# Principles: why stories work

Short summaries in our own words, each with a practical test. Authors and works are named so you can go deeper (links in `sources.md`). Treat these as lenses, not laws: no single framework fits every video.

## 1. A story is change under pressure

- **Lisa Cron** (*Wired for Story*, *Story Genius*) defines story as how events affect someone pursuing a goal that turns out to be difficult, and how that person changes internally as a result. The plot is the outer events; the story is the inner effect.
- **Robert McKee** (*Story*) says change only becomes meaningful when it is expressed through conflict.
- **Test:** can you name who changes, and what pressure forced the change?

## 2. Want, obstacle, stakes

- The audience follows a character who wants something specific. Without a goal, events are just events.
- Resistance is what makes the goal interesting. Cron stresses a deceptively difficult goal; McKee stresses forces of antagonism that push back.
- Stakes are the reason to care. **Matthew Dicks** (*Storyworthy*) treats stakes as non-negotiable: a story without them lets the listener's mind wander.
- **Test:** what does this person want, what is in the way, and what happens if they fail?

## 3. The gap between expectation and result

- McKee's central idea: tension lives in the gap between what a character expects when acting and what actually happens. Each gap forces a new choice.
- Scenes become interesting when reality does not match the plan, and the unexpected result usually matters more than the expected one.
- **Test:** where does the plan fail or surprise? If everything goes as expected, there is no scene.

## 4. Want versus need, and the inner issue

- McKee: the strongest characters also carry an unconscious desire that conflicts with the conscious one. The tension between what they want and what they need drives inner conflict.
- Cron: a "misbelief", often formed earlier in life, is what really keeps the protagonist from the goal. Outer events exist to force it into the open.
- **Will Storr** (*The Science of Storytelling*) builds characters around a flaw and notes that the brain is tuned to moments of unexpected change.
- **Test:** what did the person believe at the start that turned out to be wrong?
- **For short video:** you rarely have time for a full inner arc. One wrong belief, corrected, is plenty.

## 5. The five-second moment

- Dicks: every great story, whatever its length, is built around one tiny moment of transformation or realization. Find it, make it clear, and cut what does not lead to it.
- He suggests starting with the opposite of the end state, which creates a clear arc.
- **Test:** can you point to the exact moment something shifted? What did the person believe or do before and after?

## 6. Cause and effect: And, But, Therefore

- Randy Olson (*Houston, We Have a Narrative*) popularized ABT: "and" gives context, "but" introduces conflict, "therefore" gives consequence and resolution. He took the idea from South Park creators Trey Parker and Matt Stone, who describe replacing "and then" with "but" or "therefore" when editing.
- A chain of "and then" is a list; a chain of "but" and "therefore" is a story.
- **Test:** can you summarize the draft as one ABT sentence?

## 7. Curiosity and open loops

- **George Loewenstein**'s information-gap theory: curiosity arises when we notice a gap between what we know and what we want to know, and it feels like a mild deprivation we want to resolve.
- Some prior knowledge is needed for a gap to exist, so give the viewer something to be curious about, then withhold the rest.
- Loewenstein's triggers include posing a question, a sequence with an unknown resolution, a violated expectation, and knowing that someone else has the answer.
- **Test:** what question does the viewer carry from second 3 to the end? Is it answered?
- **Caution:** an open loop never paid off is clickbait, and it costs trust.

## 8. Transportation: why specifics and consistency matter

- **Melanie Green and Timothy Brock** describe narrative transportation: attention, emotion and imagery concentrate on the story world, and people carried away tend to take on story-consistent beliefs and resist counterarguing less.
- Anything that breaks the spell (generic language, irrelevant detours, inconsistencies) weakens the effect.
- Cron adds that the brain looks for character-centred cause and effect, and that stories are mental simulations: we feel what the protagonist feels.
- **Test:** can the viewer picture it? Does every beat belong?

## 9. Specifics: identifiable people, real emotion, one moment

- **Kindra Hall** (*Stories That Stick*) names four elements: an identifiable character, authentic emotion, a significant moment, and specific details. Her simplest structure is Normal, Explosion, New Normal.
- Emotion does not have to be dramatic; a small honest feeling beats a performed big one.
- **Test:** is there a real person, a real moment, and one detail only this story could contain?

## 10. Effort is admirable

- Pixar story artist **Emma Coats** notes that we admire a character for trying more than for succeeding, that the writer should keep the audience's interest in mind rather than their own fun, and that challenging a character with the polar opposite of what they are good at reveals them.
- Her spine, "Once upon a time... Every day... One day... Because of that... Until finally...", is a compact cause-and-effect template.
- She also advises simplifying, focusing and cutting detours, and working out the ending early.

## 11. Shapes of stories

- **Kurt Vonnegut** sketched story shapes on a good-fortune to ill-fortune axis. "Man in a Hole" is someone getting into trouble and out again, ending higher than they started.
- **Dan Harmon**'s Story Circle compresses the hero's journey into eight steps: a character in comfort, who needs something, enters an unfamiliar situation, adapts, gets what they wanted, pays a price, returns, and has changed.
- **Test:** can you draw the emotional line of the video? Does it go up and down, or is it flat?

## 12. Short-form implications

Practitioner sources (not rigorous research, so treat figures as indicative) agree on a few points:

- One idea per video. A second idea splits attention.
- Retention is shaped like a curve: the first seconds decide who stays, and mid-video drop-off happens when momentum stalls.
- Open a loop early, keep momentum through consequence ("therefore") and new gaps, close the loop at the end.
- Write for sound-off as well as sound-on viewing.

## Using these without becoming formulaic

- Choose one or two lenses that fit the content; do not run every framework on every draft.
- A true small story beats a manufactured dramatic one. Never exaggerate or invent a struggle.
- If the content really has no struggle (a pure demo, a quick tip), build tension from the viewer's problem or a surprising result instead.
